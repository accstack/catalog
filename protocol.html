1What a record is

A record is one language and what its descriptions say about the placement of stress in the word. The catalog holds 5,135 records for 5,109 languages identified by a Glottolog code; 26 records are for languages we could not match to a code and are kept under their name. A language can have more than one record when two sources describe different varieties, so counts of records run slightly above counts of languages (310 records in the strict set for 305 languages). Each record gives:

  • The language: its name, Glottolog code, family and area, with a link to its Glottolog entry.
  • Its stress type, one of: fixed stress (in the strict set, in the dominant set only, or not yet checked against the source); weight-sensitive (plainly stated, or stated tentatively); lexical; set by morphology; variable; tone or pitch accent; no word stress; phrase-level only; depends on segments; descriptions disagree; not determinable from the sources; other (see record).
  • The rule, for fixed stress: the stressed syllable for each word length, written left to right with 1 for a stressed syllable and 0 for an unstressed one, so 10 100 1000 is initial stress in words of two, three and four syllables. 1,635 records carry such a rule string. For weight-sensitive languages the record gives instead what counts as a heavy syllable, the edge the rule counts from, and what happens when no syllable is heavy.
  • The sources and the quotation: the passage from the description that decides the classification, quoted word for word with its page, with our English translation where the source is not in English; how strong that evidence is (a page image, an open full text, a publisher's text, a search snippet, or a quotation carried second-hand); every work consulted for the record; any contradiction between descriptions and any correction to a database coding, stated in full.
  • The confidence level of the classification, where one has been set (section 8), links to recordings where the language can be heard (263 records carry a clip of a few seconds, 1,106 more link to recordings elsewhere), and, for 991 records, a note from a reading of the grammar on what evidence the stress description rests on and where affixes sit relative to the root.

From this release a record also shows, each with its own quotation and citation where the source gives one: the secondary-stress statement separated from the primary rule (and a later work that revises the primary rule, where one exists); the kind of evidence the deciding source gives (impressionistic transcription, a described elicitation, instrumental measurements, a restatement of another work) and whether it presents its own data; for weight-sensitive languages what counts as heavy, whether vowel quality and geminates enter the criterion and the stress window; for tone, lexical and morphological languages the type of prominence; what affixes and clitics do to the stress; the exceptional forms the source prints; what the source says about vowel length; which variety the source describes; links to recordings; and how many of the works listed for the language have been read for a stress statement.

2Where the languages come from

The catalog began with the fixed-stress languages of two published databases: StressTyp2 (Goedemans, Heinz & van der Hulst 2015) and the appendix of Gordon (2002). Their 422 records (361 from StressTyp2, 61 from Gordon) were the first thing checked, each against the description it cites, and, with the 163 records of our August 2026 reading, they are the only records whose classification has been formalized as a rule per word length (section 5). WALS chapters 14A to 17A were used alongside them to find weight-sensitive languages and as a coding to compare against: the record says whether the WALS coding agrees with the source (158 yes, 119 partly, 18 no), and likewise for StressTyp2's coding (111 yes, 194 partly, 29 no).

Everything else comes from our own readings of descriptions, in rounds from August to September 2026. The standing goal, set on 17 September 2026, is: "For every language that exists: a complete bibliography of all relevant phonology sources; every one accessible to us read, and where the stress pattern or the prosodic type (stress / tone / pitch accent) can be determined, added to the database; the remaining sources catalogued as digital, Scan & Deliver accessible, print only, or unknown." The frame is Glottolog 5.1's 8,624 spoken languages; sign languages are excluded. (The bibliography page lists 8,629 entries: the frame plus the catalog entries whose Glottolog code is a group or dialect node rather than a single language, such as Basque, Uzbek, Sorbian and Indonesian.)

3How a record is made

Every record from our own readings went through the same three steps, and none of them was written by one reading alone. The 422 database records went a different way: each was checked once against the description its database cites (255 with no discrepancy found, 97 re-read in the editor's review with corrections and notes, 57 examined in our first check, and 13 where our check could not decide because the sources located do not settle the pattern or the cited source could not be obtained).

A first reading

The first reading finds an accessible description of the language, looking first among the copies the project already holds and then on the open web. It quotes the passage that states the stress pattern word for word, up to 1,500 characters, with the page, and saves a copy of the work so that the quotation can be checked later in the same copy. It then classifies the language and fills in the fields of the record from that passage: the rule, what counts as heavy, the domain the source describes (the word, the phrase, or both together), the exceptions the source states, and which variety the source describes.

A second, independent reading

The second reading is a separate, independent reading, given the first reading's quotation and classification and the instruction to refute them. It re-opens the saved copy at the stated page and checks that the quotation is there, word for word; that the classification follows from what the passage says; that the source describes this language and not a neighbour or a differently named variety; and that the passage is about the word rather than the phrase. It ends with one of three findings: agreed, corrected (naming the fields it changed), or rejected.

A third reading settles disagreement

Where the second reading did not simply agree, a third reading rules field by field, with reasons, after re-reading the source. The record is the first reading where the second agreed, and otherwise the third reading's ruling. In this release 2,969 records were agreed at the second reading in the September readings, 151 more from August 2026 were confirmed by a second reading and reviewed by the editor, and 1,581 were settled by a third; 1,595 records carry a note saying what the first reading got wrong and how it was corrected. Each record's "How this record was checked" line gives the date, or the period, of its own check and its outcome.

Two further independent readings of every record (20–21 September 2026)

After the release of 18 September every record was read again, twice, by readers who had not seen it before. In the second reading (20 September) a reader re-opened the saved copy of the deciding source, compared each field of the record with the passage, and proposed corrections field by field; a checker re-opened the source at the reader's locators and tried to refute each proposal, and only proposals the checker confirmed or amended were kept. In the same pass the 1,080 fixed-stress records not yet checked were graded by a reader and a checker (rule strings per word length, secondary stress, exceptions, domain, weight conditions), the 310 records of the strict set were audited against their cited source and one standard description by a reader and a checker (78 re-read by a third and fourth), secondary stress was separated from the primary rule with its own quotation and citation by a reader and a checker on 3,985 records (the completion reading supplied the status for the others), and the 434 undetermined, conflicting and variable records were re-read for a decidable type. In the third reading (20–21 September) an independent reader re-opened the deciding source of every record, wrote its own coding before looking at ours, and then compared; a checker tried to refute every disagreement; a second adjudicator re-read every change to a classification or a rule string; a blind audit re-read a seeded tenth of the records the third reader had passed. What was checked each time: the quotation at its page, that the source describes this language and the word rather than the phrase, the classification, the rule string, the exceptions the source states, the domain, and the secondary-stress statement. This release applies the confirmed corrections of both readings (3,801 field values from the second reading, 1,890 from the third, 292 from the rule grading, 7,789 from the secondary-stress reading, 357 from the re-reading of undetermined records) and the completion fields (9,638 values: identity of the variety, tone statement, exceptions, affix behaviour, exceptional forms, vowel length). Each record says under "Source re-opened by a later independent reader" whether a later reader opened a copy of its deciding work and found the passage (5,303 yes, 12 no).

Duplicates and sources that conflict

A language whose source turns out to describe a variety already on record is treated as a duplicate and set aside; one whose source is not about a distinct language is set aside likewise (105 and 44 in the last round). When the descriptions of one language disagree, the record is classified "descriptions disagree" if no description can be preferred (56 records), and otherwise keeps the preferred classification with the disagreement stated in full under "Contradictions on record" (1,494 records carry one). Contradictions are never averaged away, and a record whose only source is weak is marked with a caution (306 records).

4What is quoted and what is not

4,962 records quote the passage that decides the classification and 4,963 give its page or section. Quotations are word for word, in the language of the source; 241 records add our English translation, marked as ours. Where the passage comes from a work we could not open ourselves, the record says so: 162 records rest on a quotation carried second-hand (StressTyp2's own passages, WALS, or a secondary work quoting the description) and 257 on a search snippet only; the rest were read in a page image (1,777), an open full text (1,869) or a publisher's text (528). No evidence strength is recorded for 542 records, mostly database records checked before that field existed. For the database records and 172 others (594 records in all) the passages are given work by work under "Source passages", each marked with what it was read for; 192 of them cite more than one work.

The saved-copy rule is that a quotation counts as first-hand only when it was read in a copy we saved and the second reading found it in that same copy at the stated page. A statement that could not be re-opened is coded from the carried quotation and marked as second-hand. StressTyp2 is licensed CC BY-NC-ND; it is cited for its codings and its references, and its own text is not reproduced. On the public copy of the catalog the quoted passages are trimmed to the deciding sentences; corrections and takedown requests can be sent by opening an issue at github.com/mattschwartzscience/word-stress-catalog.

5Fixed stress: exceptions as fields, strictness as your choice

Until 18 September two sets of fixed-stress languages were adjudicated by us: a strict set (307 languages: one weight-blind rule for the word with no exceptions at the level of the rule) and a dominant set (518 languages: Gordon's 2002 looser criterion, the stated pattern is the usual one). From this release the catalog no longer draws that line for the reader. Every fixed-stress record (1,631) shows the classes of exception its source states: a hedge only (words like usually or generally with no exception named), loanwords or names, compounds or reduplications, phrase or utterance conditioning, secondary stress only, native lexical items, morphological classes or affixes, clitics or particles, and other conditions (weight, free variation, dialect). The classes come from the strict-set audit of 20 September (letters a–d on 310 records: the cited source itself states no exception and no other description does; no exception in the cited source but another description lists some; the cited source itself states exceptions; not a count-based rule on re-reading), from the rule grading of the 1,080 records not checked before (verdicts as the graders wrote them), from the third reading, and from the record's own exceptions field. On the site the filter Fixed stress: your strictness lets you tick the classes you tolerate: a language stays in your set if every exception its source states is of a ticked class. Three presets are counted in this release: strict, literal (no exception of any class, no hedge: 395 records, 395 languages, 53 distinct rule strings), strict, narrow (loans, compounds, phrase-level conditioning, secondary-only exceptions and hedges tolerated; no native lexical, morphological, clitic or other exception: 631 records, 630 languages, 72 rule strings) and dominant (any count-based rule stated as the usual pattern: 1,547 records, 1,543 languages, 109 rule strings). A record whose latest reading finds no count-based rule at all (audit letter d, or graded as weight-sensitive, lexical, morphological or undetermined: 84 records) qualifies under no preset and says why. The two sets as published on 18 September remain on every record and can be listed with the preset "Strict set as published 18 Sep".

The database codings were checked against their own sources in August 2026, starting with the 43 languages whose codings mattered most for the typological counts drawn from the catalog: each was re-read against the description its database cites. For 17 the database coding was wrong (a rule projected to word lengths the source never describes, a secondary-stress pattern the source contradicts on the quoted page, a wrong syllable), for 18 the coding depends on which of two published readings one takes, and for 8 it was confirmed. Where the two databases both cover a language, their independent codings agree on 177 of 193. Corrections were then applied one by one, each with the page that decides it; 54 records on the site carry a database correction stated in full (40 on records from StressTyp2, 14 on records from Gordon 2002).

Every exclusion was then re-judged. All 632 decisions that had removed a language or a row from the fixed-stress sets, or from the populations behind a published count, were listed with their reason and re-examined against the written rules for membership. Four languages that had been left out for a reason that referred to a theory's ability to generate the pattern rather than to the source (Dehu, Yimas, Southern Paiute, Timucua) were ruled wrongly excluded and are in; the exclusions that rested on the sources themselves stood.

In September 2026 the 412 fixed-stress records from the databases were re-read for the domain of the prominence they describe: 354 describe the word, 35 state one rule for word and phrase together, 20 give a position with no domain statement, and for 3 no description could be located. The 24 strict-set languages whose sources state the rule for word and phrase together left the strict set (it went from 329 languages to 305); they stay in the dominant set, and their record says under "Why it is outside the strict set" that the rule was stated for word and phrase together. The dominant set was not affected.

The 1,080 records labelled "fixed stress (not yet checked against the source)" on 18 September were graded on 20 September by a reader and a checker who re-read the deciding source in context and wrote the rule per word length, the secondary stresses if stated, the exceptions statement with its quotation, the domain, and a verdict (an exceptionless count-based rule, a dominant pattern with stated exceptions or hedges, or not a count-based rule); the verdict is shown on the record for information and feeds the tick boxes, and where the two graders disagreed the record says so.

6Weight-sensitive languages

Weight-sensitive languages were read in September 2026 from a list of 290 coded as weight-sensitive by StressTyp2, by WALS, or by our own earlier survey; 226 records carry that reading, and later rounds added more. A record for a weight-sensitive language states, in the source's own terms, what counts as a heavy syllable (a long vowel, a closed syllable, both, a full as against a reduced vowel, or something else), the edge of the word the rule counts from, where stress falls when no syllable is heavy, whether the domain is the word, and what the source says about exceptions. Two classifications are used: weight-sensitive where the source states the rule plainly (320 records) and weight-sensitive, source is tentative where it states it with hedges, tendencies or exceptions it does not list (379 records).

For 209 records of this reading (186 weight-sensitive and 23 with a weight-blind rule) the stated rule was also tested against the source's own example words: the words were taken from the stress section with their syllable weights and marked stress (20 for 114 records, fewer for 51, and more, up to 164, for 44), the rule was applied to each, and a second reading re-derived the weights and the stress from the source. For 64 records every example fits the rule, for 84 most do (at least 80 per cent), for 53 the examples contradict the rule as stated, and 8 give no examples. The result is shown on the record as "Examples check" and can be filtered in the catalog.

7The bibliography and the reading of every open copy

The bibliography page lists, for 8,629 languages, every work that describes or could describe the language's word stress: 136,140 works in all, from Glottolog 5.1's bibliography for each language (grammars, sketches, phonologies, and any work whose title names phonology, phonetics, sounds, syllables, tone, pitch, prosody, stress or accent: 54,586 entries over 5,468 languages), from the catalog's own records, and from works found while reading. For each work the page says how a copy can be had: digital (62,416 works), a print copy in a research library that can be scanned on request (3,459), print only (12,256), or unknown (58,009).

Works were read in several rounds. In the last, on 18 September 2026, the 7,462 works with an open copy on record that no earlier round had read were listed for reading, one language at a time, and 4,608 of the 4,613 languages listed have a result. Each work was read for a statement about stress and the statement, if there was one, was quoted with its page and related to the record already on the site as agreeing with it, refining it (adding weight, secondary stress or exceptions), or contradicting it; each quotation was then checked by a second, independent reading at the stated page. Over all rounds, 2,069 works read confirm the catalog's classification, 1,541 refine it and 344 contradict it; 4,157 new stress statements were read, 10,489 works carry notes on stress without a deciding statement, and 31,243 works read are silent on stress. 4,339 languages now have a new statement confirmed by a second reading. A further 34,717 works were judged likely to state the stress pattern but have not yet been obtained. Contradictions are kept visible on both pages as contradictions; none has been averaged into a classification.

8The confidence levels

Each language with a Glottolog code is rated on a five-level scale, defined on 18 September 2026 in these words:

very confident
clear consensus
fairly confident
low number of sources or mild disagreement but still a clear winner
moderately confident
some real disagreement but you think you know what the answer is
disputed
an active debate with authoritative sources disagreeing
not confident
basically just guessing

The level is set from the evidence on the record and in the bibliography: whether the record's own quotation is first-hand and was confirmed by a second reading, and how many independent works read agree with, refine or contradict the classification.

  • Very confident is set when the record's own confirmed first-hand source and at least one other independent first-hand work agree on the classification or refine it, and no work read contradicts it.
  • Fairly confident is set when one confirmed first-hand source stands alone, or several sources support the record with one or more contradictions that no second reading confirmed, or other works read support a record whose own quotation was not confirmed first-hand.
  • Moderately confident and disputed are never set by a rule: a language with any confirmed contradiction, any note of conflict between its sources, or a classification of "descriptions disagree", "not determinable" or "variable" is sent to a judged reading that applies the five definitions to the sources, followed by a second reading that tries to refute the level; where the two disagree the lower level stands and both are recorded.
  • Not confident is set when the record has no confirmed first-hand statement: the quotation is second-hand, the source could not be reached, or the reading was unclear, or the record has no usable word-stress statement at all.

In this release the records stand at: fairly confident 2,828; very confident 1,368; moderately confident 171; not yet rated 132; not confident 747; disputed 69. The levels of 18 September are kept; the 74 records created after that date were rated on 20 September under the same rule set, and the 106 new records of this release are not yet rated. Next to the level every record now says whether a later independent reader re-opened a copy of its deciding source and found the quoted passage there, and on how many later readings. By default the catalog shows the very confident and fairly confident languages; the other levels can be ticked in the Confidence filter.

9What we do not claim

The readings were done by language models (Claude), not by linguists: the first reading, the second and the third are all machine readings of the source, and the checks described above are checks of one machine reading against another in the same saved copy. No record is claimed to have been read by a linguist. The catalog is therefore a checked reading of what the descriptions say, with the page beside it, and not a judgement about the languages themselves. The errors we know this method makes are these:

  • a rule projected to word lengths the source never describes, or read into a source that only gives a tendency;
  • a coding that contradicts the source's own quoted passage, inherited from a database and not caught until the passage was re-read;
  • word-level and phrase-level prominence stated as one rule and coded as word stress;
  • a description matched to the wrong language or variety (the record's "Which variety" line flags the cases we know of);
  • a quotation carried second-hand, or taken from a search snippet, that the original does not bear out;
  • slips in the text of a scan, in a page number, or in our translation;
  • in the bibliography, the same work listed twice because two lists cite it differently, and access classes that have not yet been checked.

To report a mistake, open an issue at github.com/mattschwartzscience/word-stress-catalog, naming the language and, if you can, the page of the description that decides it. A correction that changes a classification is made through a written ruling that quotes the source, and the old reading stays visible on the record.

10Versions and data

The catalog and the bibliography are rebuilt from one data file whenever records change; the masthead of each page shows the date of the data release it was built from (this page describes the release of 21 September 2026), and every record shows the date, or the period, of its own check. Both pages can save the list currently on screen as a CSV file with the "Download this list as CSV" button, including the quotations, citations, contradictions and confidence levels; the bibliography's CSV includes, for each language, a link back to its entry. The public copy of the catalog is an invitation-only preview.
